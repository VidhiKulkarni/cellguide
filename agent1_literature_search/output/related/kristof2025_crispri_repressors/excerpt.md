## Abstract (structured)

L5: Background

L6: CRISPR interference (CRISPRi), the repurposing of the RNA-guided endonuclease dCas9 as a programmable transcriptional repressor, allows highly specific repression (knockdown) of gene expression. CRISPRi platforms can often have incomplete knockdown, performance variability across cell lines and gene targets, and inconsistencies dependent on the guide RNA sequence employed.

L7: Results

L8: Here, we explore the combination of novel repressor domains with strong Krüppel-associated box (KRAB) repressors, screening > 100 bipartite and tripartite fusion proteins for their ability to reduce gene expression as CRISPRi effectors. We show that these novel repressor fusions have reduced dependence on guide RNA sequences, better slow cell growth when used to knock down expression of essential genes, and function in either fusion or scaffold modalities. Furthermore, we isolate and characterize a particularly effective CRISPRi platform, dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t), which shows improved gene repression of endogenous targets both at the transcript and protein level across several cell lines and when deployed in genome-wide screens.

L9: Conclusions

L10: We posit that these novel repressor fusions can enhance the reproducibility and utility of CRISPRi in mammalian cells.

## Results

L19: Screening, constructing, and isolating potent CRISPRi repressors

L20: We first selected putative CRISPRi-compatible transcriptional repressor domains from a recent tiling library [ 39 ] that described several non-KRAB repressor domains from human proteins that had repressive activity comparable to MeCP2, the partner of KOX1(KRAB) in the gold standard dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2 CRISPRi system. Because these domains had not yet been tested for their ability to reduce gene expression in the context of a CRISPRi system (i.e., when fused to dCas9), we first tested the function of 11 high-confidence domains in a CRISPRi system. These 11 domains included one KRAB domain from KRAB domain-containing protein 1 (KRBOX1(KRAB)) [ 39 ] and 10 non-KRAB domains. We noticed that three additional transcriptional regulatory proteins, REST corepressor 1 (RCOR1), Zinc finger protein pegasus (IKZF5), and MYC associated factor X (MAX) were predicted to interact with these repressors [ 40 ], so we hypothesized that they might also be paired with dCas9 for efficient gene knockdown (Additional File 1: Supplementary Table S1).

L22: We fused each of the 14 candidate repressor domains individually to dCas9, targeted each resulting fusion protein to an SV40 promoter—enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) expression cassette with a dual-targeting sgRNA, and monitored decreasing eGFP expression levels as a correlate for transcriptional repression in HEK293T cells (Fig.  1 A, Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S1). We compared these CRISPRi repressor proteins to a dCas9 control targeted to the same promoter, as well as to previously published dCas9-KRAB domain fusions and a dCas9-MeCP2 fusion (Fig.  1 B). Many dCas9-repressor fusions exhibited improved gene knockdown compared to dCas9 alone, and three (SCMH1, CTCF, and RCOR1) exhibited improved activity compared to previously characterized MeCP2 domain (Fig.  1 B). Interestingly, a truncated MeCP2 domain (referred to here as MeCP2(t) for clarity) that was only 80 amino acids in length achieved similar levels of gene knockdown compared to the previously described 'full-length' MeCP2 repressor domain, which is 283 amino acids long [ 32 ] (Fig.  1 B). In addition, dCas9-KRBOX1(KRAB) afforded significantly improved eGFP knockdown compared to dCas9-KOX1(KRAB), further highlighting the strong transcriptional repressor activity conserved across many KRAB domains [ 41 ].

L23: We next evaluated whether attaching multiple repressor domains to dCas9 could improve gene knockdown, as was shown to occur when combining the MeCP2 domain to the KOX1(KRAB) domain [ 32 ]. Because of the importance and varying capabilities of individual KRAB domains towards gene knockdown in CRISPRi, we generated a library of bipartite repressors combining any of three KRAB domains (our newly tested KRBOX1(KRAB), the historically utilized KOX1(KRAB), and the more potent ZIM3(KRAB)), with all non-KRAB domains characterized in our initial test. In all, the size of our library of bipartite repressors was theoretically 42 variants, which we screened by testing 192 single replicates to achieve 99% coverage (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S2 A). Using our eGFP reporter assay in HEK293T cells, we observed that many bipartite repressor fusions appeared to exhibit improved capability over current gold standards, outperforming dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2 and dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB), at least during this single-replicate library screening (Fig.  1 C, Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S2 C). We next sequenced each putative repressor variant that reduced eGFP expression below the level of the dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB) control, 20 constructs in all, to determine their combination of repressor domain elements. This yielded 13 unique repressor-fusions that we then re-tested with 6 biological replicates to better gauge their gene knockdown abilities. Three novel repressor combinations (dCas9-KRBOX1(KRAB)-MAX, dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MAX, and dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2(t)), and one previously tested variant also found in our screen (dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2) [ 42 ], significantly improved gene knockdown (~ 20–30% better,  p * < 0.05) compared to dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB) in our tests in HEK293T cells (Fig.  1 D, Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S3). Analyses were performed in comparison to a dCas9 control to help differentiate between repressor-mediated expression knockdown or steric-blockade from dCas9-DNA binding, and no dual-KRAB domain fusions exhibited improved over the gold-standard repressors employing one KRAB domain (Fig.  1 D). Also, the improved gene knockdown ability of these four bipartite dCas9-repressor fusions did not correlate with their expression levels, as several had lower expression than dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB) and there was no correlation between dCas9-repressor expression and eGFP knockdown in the initial library screen (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S4A). To confirm that mCherry was an accurate proxy for quantifying dCas9-repressor expression, we performed immunofluorescence staining and observed a strong degree of overlap between mCherry and intracellular protein levels (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S5).

L24: Encouraged by the additivity of KRAB and non-KRAB repressor domains towards improving gene knockdown, we also generated a library of tripartite repressors to determine if adding an additional domain could further enhance performance. We designed this tripartite combinatorial library to fuse each of the four top-performing bipartite repressors (dCas9-KRBOX1(KRAB)-MAX, dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MAX, dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2, and dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2(t)) with a third domain (Fig.  1 E), such that our theoretical tripartite library size was 72 variants, which we screened by testing 288 single replicates to achieve 98% coverage (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S2B). We again sequenced each putative repressor variant that reduced eGFP expression below a certain threshold, in this case the level of the dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MAX bipartite variant, to determine their combination of repressor domain elements, yielding 23 tripartite domains to retest in replicate. One novel tripartite fusion (dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MAX-MeCP2(t)) exhibited superior knockdown efficiency compared to all four potent bipartite variants in HEK293T cells (~ 10% better,  p * < 0.05) (Fig.  1 F). Three other novel tripartite repressors (dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2(t)-MeCP2(t), dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2(t)-KOX1(KRAB), and dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MAX-IRF2BP1) had superior gene knockdown compared to gold standard controls (* p  < 0.05) and also outperformed three of four bipartite domains, suggesting that addition of repressor domains continues to enhance knockdown efficacy but may be reaching its limit, at least using our eGFP reporter assay (Fig.  1 F). In addition, the improved knockdown mediated by these tripartite domains again did not appear to correlate with expression levels of the dCas9-repressor fusions (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S4 A). For comparison, sequences of our single, bipartite, and tripartite repressor domain fusions as well as previously described gold standards dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB) and dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2 repressors can be found in Additional File 1: Supplementary Table S1. The gold standard repressor domains we utilized for comparative analyses to our variants had identical protein sequences to those developed previously [ 32 ,  35 ].

L27: As our library results suggested that (1) further addition of domains to our combinatorial repressor constructs may fail to increase their potency and (2) the highly potent repressor fusions we have highlighted (4 bipartite and 1 tripartite) appeared to outperform gold standard CRISPRi repressor proteins, we next sought to evaluate a subset these fusions more fully. Despite recent advances in sgRNA design and activity prediction [ 43 ], a continuing challenge for CRISPRi is that performance can be significantly impacted (and reduced) by the selected sgRNA sequence [ 44 ]. To determine whether our engineered repressors reduce the variance in function arising from sgRNA sequence, we constructed a panel of sgRNAs tiling our SV40 promoter – eGFP reporter cassette, and then we used it to compare sgRNA-associated variability mediated by prior domains and our novel repressors. These sgRNAs targeted template and non-template strands, as well as both upstream and downstream of the eGFP transcription start site (TSS) (Additional File 1: Supplementary Table S2). Promisingly, we saw that dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MAX-MeCP2(t) showed significantly improved repression compared to dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB) for 8 out of 9 sgRNAs of our panel (Fig.  2 A). The improved gene knockdown occurred regardless of both target position and DNA strand, indicating that that the addition of the MAX and MeCP2(t) repressor domains enhanced the knockdown ability of the gold standard dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB) protein in this context. It also suggests that our variants' high activity was unlikely to be caused by the dual-targeting reporter sgRNA used in our preliminary screens. Furthermore, we observed that our repressors demonstrated greater improvement in gene repression when utilizing sgRNAs that had poorer functionality for previously described CRISPRi effectors, suggesting that our optimized dCas9-repressor fusions helped to mitigate variance from less effective sgRNAs (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S6).

L28: We next sought to determine if our top-performing dCas9-repressor fusions could outperform the gold standard CRISPRi effectors when targeting endogenous genes, as there is also variability in performance of CRISPRi effectors on a gene-by-gene basis [ 44 ]. We co-transfected three top-performing novel variants, dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MAX, dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2(t), and dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MAX-MeCP2(t) into HEK293T cells with sgRNAs targeting one of four endogenous genes and then quantified gene knockdown using quantitative PCR with reverse transcription (RT-qPCR) in successfully transfected cells (sgRNA + /dCas9-repressor + ). The four genes selected for this assay were BLM, CANX, SEL1L, and SYVN1, as they have been used previously when characterizing established CRISPRi systems [ 32 ,  35 ], and we compared our novel variants to previously published gold-standard CRISPRi systems. The dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) and dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MAX-MeCP2(t) effectors induced the strongest gene knockdown across all four loci tested (Fig.  2 B) and reduced heterogeneity in CRISPRi activity compared to the dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB) gold standard (e.g., between 40 and 75% knockdown for ZIM3(KRAB) versus between 65 and 80% knockdown for ZIM3(KRAB)-MAX-MeCP2(t)). Some residual variation in degree of gene knockdown is to be expected and may originate from individual sgRNA binding dynamics or local chromatin architecture, as has been described previously [ 45 ,  46 ].

L31: Next, we sought to evaluate the efficacy of our novel repressor fusions by targeting essential genes, genes required for sustained cell growth and survival [ 47 ], and quantifying associated phenotype and gene expression changes. To do so, we utilized transduced cell lines to remove potential artifacts associated with transfection-based assays. We generated A549 cells constitutively expressing dCas9-repressor fusions (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S7) and used competitive growth assays to measure proliferation rates when targeting specific genes. We also generated A549 cells expressing the dCas9 protein or tagBFP as controls. We transduced each dCas9-repressor-expressing A549 cell population with lentiviral cassettes bearing a single sgRNA in addition to an eGFP fluorescent marker labeling sgRNA-expressing cells (Fig.  3 A).

L32: In separate experiments, we targeted three different genes using three distinct sgRNAs each: (i) mitochondrial co-chaperone DNAJC19 (highly essential), (ii) GTPase and oncogene KRAS (moderately essential), and (iii) small ribosomal subunit protein MRPS11 (marginally essential) [ 47 ]. DNAJC19 was chosen for this study because it was employed in previous characterization of dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB) [ 34 ]. Following transductions yielding ~ 50% sgRNA/eGFP expressing cell populations, we monitored the representation of eGFP-positive cells to determine if cells bearing repressor domains might see accelerated depletion of sgRNA (eGFP) expressing cells (Fig.  3 A). A549 cells expressing dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) experienced the highest levels of depletion across all tested sgRNAs targeting each essential gene, highlighting its improved repressive capability over current gold-standards dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB) and dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2 (Fig.  3 B-D, Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S8). To further link phenotypic effects from CRISPRi perturbation to individual gene knockdown efficiencies, we quantified RNA expression levels for all three essential target genes using RT-qPCR. Importantly, the dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) variant showed the strongest repression for 7 out of 9 targeting sgRNAs, correlating well with proliferation rates observed in A549 cells, and validating improved transcriptional repression mediated by this bipartite protein in a second cell line of a different lineage (lung & embryonic kidney) (Fig.  3 B-D).

L33: Because individual repressor domains fused to dCas9 for CRISPRi-mediated gene silencing might recruit factors co-regulating global transcriptional programs, their overexpression might introduce undesired, non-specific effects on cellular functions or growth, as was recently demonstrated for the CRISPRi effector SID4X-dCas9-KOX1(KRAB) [ 34 ,  48 ]. To determine if our new dCas9-repressor fusions influenced cellular proliferation, we measured relative growth rates of cell lines with integrated and constitutively expressed variants using a normalized co-culture assay (Fig.  3 E). We seeded dCas9-repressor-expressing A549 cell lines in a ~ 1:1 ratio with A549 cells engineered with eGFP only (A549-eGFP), and then used flow cytometry to measure the representation of eGFP-positive cells over time [ 43 ]. A549-eGFP cells served as an internal control to normalize the impact of cellular engineering and subsequent selection, and to aid in assessing potential effects of silencing on dropout rates of effector-expressing cells. Cell lines harboring most repressor variants proliferated at a similar rate as A549-eGFP control cells, suggesting that long-term expression of the repressor fusions is not toxic (Fig.  3 F). Interestingly, stable integration of dCas9-KOX1(KRAB) (~ 1.7% per day) and SID4x-dCas9-KOX1(KRAB) (~ 1% per day) exhibited slight increases in growth compared to A549-eGFP control cells. This is contrary to previous work in K562 cells [ 34 ], which reported negligible effects for dCas9-KOX1(KRAB) and significant proliferation losses (~ 6% per day) for SID4x-dCas9-KOX1(KRAB).

L36: CRISPRi systems typically employ direct fusion or repressor proteins to dCas9, however, alternative repressor recruitment strategies can be employed [ 49 – 51 ]. To determine if our novel repressor protein fusions could still mediate gene knockdown in a scaffold-based effector recruitment system, we fused our top-performing repressors to the PP7 capsid protein (PCP), utilized sgRNAs encoding PP7 aptamers, and then compared this targeting approach with direct dCas9-repressor fusions (Fig.  4 A). Our top repressors recruited via PCP-PP7 aptamer binding still outperformed prior best-in-class effectors, although they were less effective towards reducing protein expression than their corresponding dCas9 fusions, particularly for KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) and ZIM3(KRAB)-MAX-MeCP2(t) (Fig.  4 B). The scaffold recruitment and direct fusion approaches did perform comparably when dCas9 was targeted downstream of the eGFP reporter gene's TSS. This result may originate from the PCP-PP7 system recruiting up to four PCP-repressor fusions to dCas9 simultaneously, increasing potential steric hindrance of RNA polymerase progression more than corresponding dCas9 fusions.

L37: Investigating domain order and necessity – isolation of another promising repressor

L38: We next sought to evaluate the necessity of each individual domain towards the ability of the ZIM3(KRAB)-MAX-MeCP2(t) tripartite repressor to reduce gene expression, as well as the impact of domain order in this dCas9-based fusion, as domain order can impact function [ 52 ]. We designed constructs fusing dCas9 to all possible combinatorial fusions of ZIM3(KRAB), MAX, and MeCP2(t) and assayed their gene expression knockdown activity in HEK293T cells using two different sgRNAs (Fig.  4 C). Contrary to previous work characterizing multi-component transcriptional activator fusions [ 52 ], we observed little change in CRISPRi activity when altering domain order across bipartite and tripartite fusions. Interestingly, we saw that the fusion dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) exhibited improved knockdown ability compared to dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB), as well as comparable activity to the tripartite repressor from which it was derived, so we included the bipartite dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) repressor in following studies.

L39: Highly efficient knockdown of endogenous loci with dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t)

L40: To determine the cell-line dependent performance of these new repressors, we conducted side-by-side comparisons and our novel repressors, now including the dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) variant, and gold-standard effectors in 6 diverse mammalian cell lines: A549 (human lung adenocarcinoma), CHO-K1 (Chinese hamster ovary), HCT116 (human colon carcinoma), HeLa (human cervical carcinoma), Neuro2A (mouse neuroblast), and NIH-3T3 (mouse embryonic fibroblast). We saw that dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) exhibited the strongest gene knockdown across all cell lines tested when using our eGFP reporter co-transfection assay (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S9). Our two most potent repressors, dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) and dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2(t), outperformed dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB) by an average of 50% and 31%, respectively, across cell lines (* p  < 0.05 or ** p  < 0.01), showing strong gene suppression activity across cell backgrounds.

L42: However, we also observed that MAX-containing repressor variants functioned markedly worse in other cell lines compared to their performance in HEK293T cells (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S10 A). Analysis of dCas9-repressor expression levels suggests that this discrepancy did not result from poor effector expression of the MAX containing repressors (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S10B). Consequently, we hypothesized that this notable cell-to-cell variability originates from the context-dependent regulatory activity of MAX through its recruited co-factors. There are nine high-confidence co-factors that dimerize with MAX [ 53 ,  54 ], which of three facilitate transcriptional activation (MYC, MYCL, MYCN) and six mediate transcriptional repression (MGA, MNT, MXD1, MXI1, MXD3, MXD4) (Fig.  5 D) [ 55 ,  56 ]. Using transcriptomics datasets, we quantified levels of the MAX-binding co-factors in A549, HCT116, and HeLa cells, three lines in which MAX harboring fusions performed significantly worse than their 'non-MAX' counterparts [ 57 ] (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S10 A). HeLa cells had particularly high expression of MYC, and while A549 and HCT116 cells had lower MYC expression, they also had much lower relative expression of repression-mediating co-factors when normalized by MYC expression level (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S10 C-E). Therefore, we surmise that either high MYC expression or a low relative expression of repressive cofactors (e.g., MXD3) may be reducing the function of MAX-containing CRISPRi effectors outside of the HEK293T cell line that we originally used for combinatorial library screenings.

L43: To expand our preliminary studies using SV40-eGFP reporter assays, we next sought to verify whether three of our novel effectors, dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t), dCas9- KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2(t), and dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MAX-MeCP2(t), could enhance transcriptional silencing of endogenous genes across four cell lines, A549, HCT116, HEK293T, and HeLa cells. When directed toward endogenous coding genes, dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) demonstrated significantly improved gene knockdown compared to dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB) and dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2 in all four cell lines and across almost all target genes, surpassing even our highly effective dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) variant in several instances (Fig.  5 A). Paralleling our observations with eGFP reporter assays (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S9), dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MAX-MeCP2(t) achieved poor gene knockdown in cell lines apart from HEK293T (Fig.  5 A), further demonstrating this tripartite variant's cell line context-dependent activity.

L44: Next, we sought to demonstrate transcriptional repression of non-coding endogenous genes, so we performed similar knockdown comparisons with two lncRNA genes, MINCR and DANCR (with two distinct sgRNAs each), in HEK293T cells (Fig.  5 B). For these non-coding transcripts, dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) achieved more efficient knockdown than dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB) and dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2 for 3 out of 4 tested sgRNAs (Fig.  5 B). To further investigate the degree to which our top-performing variants regulate protein expression levels, we also evaluated their ability to reduce the display of two endogenous cell surface markers, CD81 and CD151, in four different cell lines (Fig.  5 C, Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S11), and employing previously characterized 'strong' and 'weak' sgRNA sequences for each gene [ 34 ]. As expected, dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) significantly outperformed dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB) for most tested conditions (12 out of 14) across the different cell lines (Fig.  5 C, Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S11). Like our observations when evaluating CRISPRi platforms across a panel of SV40-eGFP-targeting sgRNAs (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S6), dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) exhibited the most notable performance improvements with weaker sgRNAs, suggesting that implementation of this novel platform could reduce guide-dependent effects across a broader set of target genes.

L47: Pooled genome-wide screening is a powerful application of CRISPR technology, enabling large-scale interrogation of gene-phenotype relationships and genes regulating fundamental biological processes [ 58 ]. To determine how our most powerful effector, dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t), performs compared to other field-standard CRISPRi tools in the context of pooled genome-wide screens, we performed unbiased dropout screening in HCT116 cells. After generating heterogeneous populations of HCT116 cells stably expressing dCas9, dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB), dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2, or dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t), we transduced these cells with the Dolcetto Set A sgRNA library [ 36 ], measured changes in sgRNA representation over a 21-day outgrowth period using high-throughput sequencing [ 59 ], and then calculated the fold depletion of sgRNAs. We then used two distinct analyses to gauge how well each CRISPRi platform functioned towards mediating negative selections of genes essential for cell growth. In one analysis (Fig.  6 A), we looked at the level of depletion of previously identified 215 core essential and 542 non-essential genes [ 60 ]. These core essential and non-essential genes are known to be important, or expendable, respectively, for cell growth across cancer cell lines (Additional File 1: Supplementary Table S3) [ 60 ]. Cells expressing dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) demonstrated the strongest depletion (negative selection) of core essential genes without depletion of sgRNAs targeting the core non-essential genes (Fig.  6 A, Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S12 A). In our second analysis (Fig.  6 B), we analyzed depletion rates for every targeted gene in our genome-wide sgRNA library (18,901 genes in all) (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S12B) [ 36 ]. Using the conventional MAGeCK analysis pipeline [ 61 ] implemented in previous CRISPRi screens [ 32 ,  35 ], we identified genes experiencing significant depletion during the 21-day outgrowth period (with a MAGeCK beta score < −0.3 and Wald-adjusted  p -values < 0.05) to evaluate the ability of dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) to reveal genes essential for cell growth, again comparing to previous gold-standard repressors. Through this analysis, genome-wide CRISPRi screening with dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) uncovered a total of 1,719 essential genes, significantly outperforming both dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB) (696 essential genes) and dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2 (636 essential genes) (Fig.  6 B). Strikingly, the number of depleted genes found with dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) closely aligns with the number of essential genes (~ 1,800) reported from previous CRISPR-Cas9 screens in HCT116 cells [ 62 ,  63 ].

L48: Recruitment of chromatin modifiers has been shown to enact significant epigenetic changes across vast genomic distances [ 64 ,  65 ]. Furthermore, previous work comparing various CRISPRi tools reported that repressor fusions incorporating the canonical, full-length MeCP2 repressor domain (i.e., dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2) can induce non-specific alteration of gene expression and cell proliferation [ 34 ]. Indeed, these factors could significantly skew the results of large-scale CRISPRi screens [ 66 ]. Because of this, we sought to further investigate the targeting specificity of dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t). Following previous methodology used during development of dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2 [ 32 ], we targeted our CRISPRi effectors to SYVN1 in HEK293T cells and then measured relative RNA abundances of SYVN1 and SYVN1's five additional nearest-neighbor genes: ZNHIT2, FAU, MRPL49, SPDYC, and CAPN1 (Fig.  6 C). We found that dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) achieved the highest on-target knockdown of SYVN1 (comparable to dCas9-KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) and dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MAX-MeCP2(t)) without significantly altering expression of any nearest-neighbor gene (Fig.  6 C).

L51: Protein linkers have been shown to substantially influence the activity of several chimeric fusion proteins [ 67 ]. To determine whether linker composition can influence the potency of dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t)-mediated gene suppression, we generated variants of dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) containing several classes and lengths (i.e., repeats) of well-established protein–protein linkers [ 68 ] placed between the ZIM3(KRAB) and MeCP2(t) subdomains (Additional File 1: Supplementary Table S4). We compared the knockdown efficiency of all these linker-variants with the 'original' dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) effector using eGFP reporter assays in both HEK293T and HCT116 cell lines (Fig.  7 ). Overall, variants harboring the modified linkers achieved comparable performance to the original dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) fusion. Interestingly, the variant utilizing a single rigid linker, dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-(EAAAK) 1 -MeCP2(t), showed improved gene suppression over both dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB) and dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) for 5 out of 6 conditions across both cell lines (Fig.  7 ). Although most linker modifications do not significantly impact the performance of dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t), our results support the idea that, in addition to subdomain order and identity, engineering linker sequences is another important optimization tool for CRISPRi effector activity.

## Discussion

L52: Discussion

L53: When we conceived this study, we sought to address key limitations hindering the application of CRISPRi technology to a wider scope of biological studies, namely the limitations resulting from: (1) incomplete gene suppression, (2) context-dependent behavior in different cell lines and, (3) performance variability depending on genomic loci and sgRNA sequence. Overall, the novel transcriptional repressor fusions we developed, particularly dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t), achieve these original aims. In this work, we showed that dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t), and several other novel bipartite and tripartite CRISPRi systems, help reduce gene knockdown variability by across different sgRNA targeting sequences (Figs.  2 A,  5 C), targeting various genes of interest (Fig.  2 B), and working across multiple cell lines (Fig.  5 A). In addition to significantly enhancing target gene suppression across genomic loci and cellular contexts, these performance improvements enabled improved perturbation of gene-phenotype relationships, both for individual essential genes (Fig.  3 B-D) and at genome-wide library scales (Fig.  6 B).

L54: In the process of developing and characterizing dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t), we introduced a multi-pronged engineering paradigm for optimizing CRISPRi tools incorporating the following approaches: (1) effector domain characterization (Fig.  1 B), (2) protein subdomain switching libraries (Fig.  1 C), (3) domain re-configuration (Fig.  4 C), (4) validation across different cell lines (Fig.  5 A, Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S9), and (5) protein–protein linker sequence optimization (Fig.  7 ). Contrary to previous results for CRISPR-based transcriptional activators [ 49 ,  69 ], we observed that PP7/PCP-based scaffold recruitment of our top-performing effector domain combinations, KOX1(KRAB)-MeCP2(t) and ZIM3(KRAB)-MAX-MeCP2(t), slightly underperformed their corresponding direct dCas9 fusions (Fig.  4 B). Further optimization efforts, such as domain-switching libraries, may be worthwhile to determine whether scaffold recruitment modalities could meet (or exceed) the performance of direct dCas9-repressor fusions, as scaffold recruitment is an enabling technique for dCas9-based multiplexed gene activation and repression [ 70 ].

L55: While a potential weakness of our study is that we routinely utilized an eGFP-based reporter assay and transient transfections of mammalian cell lines, we observed that the functionality of our top-performing repressors was consistent when targeting endogenous loci at both the protein (Fig.  5 C, Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S11) and RNA levels (Fig.  5 A) in four different cell lines. We further confirmed the function of these repressors after their integration into cell lines via phenotypic analysis associated with essential gene knockdown (Fig.  3 B-D, Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S8) and whole-genome CRISPRi screening (Fig.  6 A-B). Whereas the activity of these proteins might be expected to strongly correlate with their expression level, we often saw that that was not the case (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S4 A – S4 C, Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S5). These results support the concept that it is the intrinsic properties of the protein subdomains (and their potentially additive mechanisms of action) that bestow their improved function. In fact, the tested tripartite repressor fusions almost exclusively had equivalent or higher expression in HEK293T cells than canonical dCas9-repressor combinations (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S4B), but only a single tripartite variant exceeded the activity of the two domain fusions (Fig.  1 F). Still, the construction and screening of these domain fusion libraries, of which the combined theoretical size surpassed 100 unique variants, allowed isolation of ~ 10 tri- and ~ 6 bipartite repressor variants with activity on par with or surpassing the repressive ability of dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB) when tested with our eGFP reporter assay (Fig.  1 D, F), confirming the utility of combinatorial protein libraries for generating potent tools for targeted transcriptional control. Interestingly, we did not isolate our eventual top-performing repressor variant, dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t), from our original bipartite library screening process despite it being a potential library member and screening to achieve a theoretical 99% coverage. While this 'near miss' may have resulted from simply not having screened for this variant originally, it may have also resulted from an over-reliance on an  n  = 1 library screening strategy that can be hampered by inherent biological variability. In addition, it demonstrates the importance of more thorough and rational engineering strategies towards isolating best-performing protein fusion candidates, as we saw when we were able to rectify our 'near miss' by while fully dissecting the tripartite dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MAX-MeCP2(t) variant (Fig.  4 C).

L56: The superior performance of bipartite and tripartite effector combinations, described in this work and also for CRISPR-based transcriptional activators [ 52 ,  69 ], likely results from combining distinct, yet complimentary, mechanisms for modifying local epigenetic signatures. However, our understanding of how these individual repressor domains mechanistically work together to silence gene expression remains ambiguous. KRAB domains, known for their near-ubiquitous strong repressive activity, are almost exclusively used in CRISPRi applications in mammalian cells. KRAB domains largely (through not always) effectively modulate transcription by interacting with TRIM28/KAP1 [ 71 ]. Although our screening efforts revealed that KRAB domains' relatively strong gene knockdown efficiency can be enhanced through addition of MeCP2 or MAX in HEK293T cells (both likely recruiting the SIN3A-histone deacetylase complex [ 72 ]), no other domains conferred additional benefit when combined with KRAB-domains (Fig.  1 D). In addition, no dual-KRAB domain fusions exhibited improved activity over repressors employing the strong ZIM3-KRAB domain (Fig.  1 D). These results suggest that KRAB-induced gene silencing may have few accessible synergistic mechanisms that can augment their function, while also illustrating the importance of recruiting complimentary secondary domains to enhance gene knockdown. The importance of cofactor recruitment was also demonstrated by our initial experiment characterizing putative CRISPRi single domain repressor variants (Fig.  1 B), in which fusion of the regulatory proteins RCOR1 or MAX to dCas9, and therefore direct recruitment, yielded functional CRISPRi systems.

L57: The discovery and characterization of new repressor domains is still a promising avenue for developing optimized CRISPRi systems. Furthermore, it could further our understanding of how natural transcription factors cooperatively regulate gene expression [ 73 ]. Recent studies exploring transcription factor subdomains are valuable resources for developing CRISPRi systems with potentially diverse mechanisms of action, modes of temporal control, and activity levels [ 39 ,  74 – 76 ]. Here, we demonstrate this utility by testing a small panel of diverse CRISPRi-compatible, non-KRAB domains with comparable repression efficiency as MeCP2. It would be instructive to significantly expand our panel of effective dCas9-compatible, non-KRAB dCas9-repressor domains, build a comprehensive understanding of their individual activities and co-factor identities, and construct multi-domain libraries to permit discovery of additional high-activity repressor combinations. Incorporating knowledge of recruited co-factors, cooperative epigenetic modifications, and repressor-co-factor affinities would also enable greater means of rational design, permitting future development of CRISPRi effector panels with well-characterized, diverse gene silencing efficiency and kinetics. One recent example of this approach is the elegant CRISPRoff system, which combined dCas9-KOX1(KRAB) with subunits of epigenetic modifiers DNMT3 A and DNMT3L to achieve long-term, heritable silencing across most gene targets [ 77 ]. We envision that future studies exploring complimentary epigenetic silencing mechanisms, standardizing optimal sgRNAs between different CRISPRi approaches, and investigating overall effector performance criteria, such as gene knockdown efficiency and duration, could together help overcome potential 'upper limits' on KRAB-based CRISPRi system performance.

L58: One major factor determining transcription factor activity is the function of key effector domains [ 78 ], and emerging work has begun exploring the context-dependent behavior of these domains by quantifying their affinities for recruited co-factors [ 79 ]. The variable activity of MAX-containing bipartite and tripartite variants across different cell lines (Additional File 2: Supplementary Figure S10) demonstrates the importance of considering cell–cell differences in co-transcription factor expression before selecting CRISPRi repressors for a given application. Although experimental validation, such as protein–protein interaction analysis, is necessary to confirm the mechanistic basis of our observations with MAX-containing variants, the observation of how their performance varies across cell lines verifies the importance of full characterization of transcriptional effectors across cells lines. Additional work correlating endogenous co-factor (and dCas9-repressor fusion) expression levels across a broad panel of mammalian cell lines could help clarify mechanisms underpinning cell-to-cell variability of CRISPRi tools. We envision that computational models could incorporate transcriptomic datasets to predict optimal repressor domain (and combinations thereof) for a given cell line. Further study of protein–protein interactions between CRISPRi effectors and endogenous transcriptional regulators may also reveal the cause of non-specific growth alterations when employing CRISPRi systems. Our competition assay results suggest that our engineered variants do not induce cellular toxicity in A549 cells (Fig.  3 F), however, additional investigation is required to determine how CRISPRi effector expression impacts cell function and global transcriptional regulation in a cell line-dependent manner. Beyond their importance in constructing enhanced gene regulation tools, however, characterization of fused transcriptional effectors could also further our understanding of how natural transcription factors cooperatively regulate gene expression [ 73 ].

---

## Conclusions

L59: Conclusions

L60: Together, this work introduces a holistic approach for optimizing CRISPRi tools for directed, site-specific transcriptional control in mammalian cells. We demonstrate that performing subdomain switching libraries, exploring domain configuration, recognizing the context-dependent nature of transcriptional silencing, and optimizing linker composition could be implemented together to construct novel CRISPRi platforms with best-in-class performance, most notably, dCas9-ZIM3(KRAB)-MeCP2(t). We anticipate that this highly effective domain fusion, and our effector engineering approach, will aid a wide range of biological applications and accelerate the development of the next generation of CRISPR-based tools for robust transcriptional control in mammalian cells.

## Methods (selected subsections)

L64: Plasmid construction for CRISPRi repressors and sgRNAs

L65: Individual repressor domains for this study (Additional File 1: Supplementary Table S1) were acquired by PCR-amplification from a single-strand cDNA library. In short, total RNA from 10 7  HEK293T cells was first purified using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) and reverse-transcribed using a SuperScript VILO cDNA Synthesis Kit (Invitrogen). Repressor domains were then PCR-amplified from this first-strand cDNA pool using KOD Hot Start Polymerase (Novagen) using 250 ng of cDNA product per reaction with cycling conditions in-line with the manufacturer's protocol.

L66: CRISPRi dCas9-repressor fusion plasmids for transient expression were constructed by inserting individual repressor domains into a custom Golden Gate compatible base vector (pEF1α-dCas9-mCherry) derived from the plasmid pSMART-sgRNA (Addgene #80,427). This custom base vector, constructed via Gibson Assembly from the pSMART backbone digested with BamHI and XbaI (New England Biolabs), contains the EF1α promoter driving expression of human codon-optimized  Streptococcus pyogenes  dCas9 (with 1 N-terminal and 2 C-terminal SV40 NLS elements), a C-terminal GS-rich linker with Esp3I restriction sites allowing insertion of various effector domains, and a P2A-mCherry marker enabling quantification of expression levels via flow cytometry. Golden Gate Assembly was employed for cloning single, bipartite, and tripartite dCas9-repressor fusions for analysis, and the repressors were fused to the C-terminus of dCas9.

L67: Constructs enabling stable integration of CRISPRi repressors were derived from an in-house custom base vector (pLV-dCas9-tagBFP). Briefly, this base vector uses a spleen focus-forming virus (SFFV) promoter with an upstream ubiquitous chromatin-opening element (UCOE) to drive expression of  Streptococcus pyogenes  dCas9, internal SV40 NLS tags, a G/S-rich linker with Esp3I restriction sites to enable insertion of additional repressor domains, and a C-terminal tagBFP fluorescent marker linked via a T2A self-cleaving peptide. This plasmid was built by PCR-amplifying all requisite parts and inserting them with Gibson Assembly into lentiviral backbone pLeGO-C (Addgene #27,348) linearized by digestion with XbaI and EcoRI (New England Biolabs). From this base vector, Golden Gate Assembly was used for building all lentiviral dCas9-repressor fusion constructs, and these vectors were transformed via electroporation into NEB Stable  E. Coli  (New England Biolabs) to prevent plasmid recombination during subsequent cloning steps.

L68: The sgRNAs targeting either eGFP or individual endogenous genes for transient expression were cloned into a vector (pSMART-sgRNA-SV40-eGFP) constructed by adding the SV40-eGFP cassette via Gibson Assembly into the pSMART-sgRNA backbone. Following guide design, individual sgRNA constructs were made by annealing two complimentary oligonucleotides (Eurofins Genomics) containing the full sgRNA sequences and appropriate overhangs, then ligating the oligo product with pSMART-sgRNA-SV40-eGFP backbone pre-digested with Esp3I (New England Biolabs). Constructs for sgRNA integration were cloned using the identical ligation method into a custom lentiviral vector (pLV-sgRNA-EF1α-eGFP-T2 A-PuroR) originally made by inserting PCR-amplified EF1α, eGFP, and puromycin resistance marker within pXPR_050 (Addgene #96,925) linearized by digestion with MluI and XmaI (New England Biolabs).

L87: CRISPRi screening library preparation and titer estimation

L88: CRISPRi screens were performed using the Dolcetto Set A sgRNA library (Addgene Cat# 92,385). The plasmid DNA library was amplified using the depositor's protocol with several modifications. In brief, 500 ng of the pooled plasmid library was transformed into 300uL ElectroMAXTMSTBL4 competent cells (Invitrogen Cat# 11,635,018) using 6 separate cuvettes (Fisher Scientific Cat# FB101) and immediately recovered in pre-warmed SOC media (New England Biolabs Cat# B9020S) for 1 h at 37 °C. After recovery, all cells were pooled together, mixed well, and then equally split into 3 separate 100 mL LB-ampicillin cultures for overnight growth. The next day, plasmid DNA was isolated and purified from the overnight cultures via Maxiprep (Qiagen Cat# 12,163) adhering to the manufacturer's instructions. Following library amplification, library lentiviral preps were generated using a scaled-up virus production protocol (as described in the Lentivirus Production subsection). HEK293T cells were seeded in 12 individual 15 cm dishes at a density of 12,000,000 cells/dish. 24 h later, cells were transfected with complexes prepared with the following components (after a 20 min formation time): 36 mL room-temperature Opti-MEM, 180 mg pooled plasmid library, 144 mg pSPAX2, 36 mg pMD2.G, and 1080 mL of TransIT-LT1. 48 h post-transfection, supernatants were collected into several 50 mL conical tubes, centrifuged at 1000 × g to remove cellular debris, and passed through 0.45 mm filter units (Avantor Cat# 10,040–466). After this, the lentivirus was precipitated using PEG-it Virus Precipitation Solution (System Biosciences Cat# LV825 A-1) according to the manufacturer's protocol, concentrated in ice-cold PBS, and subsequently stored at −80 °C until use.

L89: To determine lentiviral titer required for at-scale spin transductions, all cell lines were first seeded in 12-well plates (Corning Cat# 3513) at a density of 3,000,000 cells/well in media containing 8 mg/mL polybrene (Millipore-Sigma Cat# TR-1003). Lentivirus was next added at various volumes (0 mL, 1 mL, 2.5 mL, 5 mL, 10 mL, 25 mL) to duplicate wells. Plates were then centrifuged at 1000 × g for 2 h at 33 °C and stored in the 37 °C incubator overnight. On the following morning, cells from each well were trypsinized, counted, and transferred to 8 independent wells of a 96-well plate at 8,000 cells/well. For each condition (8 wells), cells in 4 wells received 2 mg/mL puromycin, whereas the other 4 wells contained no antibiotic. 48 h later (i.e., when cells cultured without puromycin reached ~ 90% confluence), relative cell numbers were quantified using the RealTime-Glo MT Cell Viability Assay (Promega Cat #G9711). MOI was computed by dividing the average luminescence for cells grown under puromycin selection by the average luminescence from cells cultured without puromycin.

L90: Genome-wide CRISPRi screening

L91: HCT116 cell lines stably expressing various CRISPRi effectors (or wild-type control cells) were first seeded at 3,000,000 cells/well in 12-well plates with 8 mg/mL polybrene for at-scale transduction. To maintain sufficient library coverage of at least ~ 500 cells/sgRNA, 108,000,000 cells (i.e., 3 separate 12-well plates) were transduced with the Dolcetto lentiviral library at MOI ~ 0.3 for each biological replicate. Following virus addition, plates were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 2 h at 33 °C, then transferred to the 37 °C incubator for overnight growth. The next day, transduced cells were pooled together based on replicate conditions and transferred into T300 flasks (Avantor Cat# 10,062–886) with 2 mg/mL puromycin. After 96 h of puromycin selection, cells were repeatedly passaged every 2–3 days at densities required to maintain at least a 500-fold library coverage. At screen initiation (immediately following puromycin selection) and endpoint (21 days later), 50,000,000 cells for all biological replicates (2 per condition) were pelleted, frozen promptly, and stored at −20 °C prior to genomic DNA extraction.

L92: NGS library preparation and analysis

L93: gDNA was extracted from all HCT116 cell pellets using a modified salt precipitation protocol [ 82 ]. Briefly, pellets were lysed in 6 mL gDNA Lysis Buffer (1% SDS, 5 mM EDTA, and 5 mM, pH = 8.0) and 30µL proteinase K (Qiagen Cat# RP107B-5), thoroughly vortexed, and then incubated at 55 °C overnight. Next, 30 mL of 10 mg/mL RNase A (Millipore Sigma Cat# 10,109,142,001, dissolved in gDNA Lysis Buffer) was added, and after inverting 25 times, lysates were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After subsequent cooling on ice, 7.5 M ice-cold ammonium acetate was next added to the lysates, and following centrifugation to isolate supernatants, 6 mL of pure IPA was added to precipitate gDNA out of solution. After centrifugation, gDNA pellets were washed with 70% EtOH, air-dried until slightly translucent (~ 45 min), and resuspended in 500µL TE Buffer (Fisher Scientific Cat# BP24731) at 65 °C for 1 h, and quantified via UV/Vis (NanoDrop) prior to downstream processing.

L94: Following gDNA isolation, amplicon NGS libraries were generated through a two-step PCR amplification protocol, where the first PCR (PCR1) amplifies the diverse library sgRNAs with primers recognizing conserved lentiviral integration sequences and the second PCR (PCR2) appending PCR1 products with Illumina adapters (P5 and P7) and unique dual indices (i5 and i7) (Additional File 1: Supplementary Table S6). Based on the approximate gDNA yield of 7 mg gDNA/1 × 10 6  cells and required representation of 500 cells/sgRNA to maintain library coverage, ~ 250 mg of extracted gDNA per condition was amplified across 48 individual 50 mL PCR reactions. PCR1 reactions were prepared on ice with the following per-well composition: (1) 5.5 mg (i.e. 5.5 mL of 1 mg/mL normalized) gDNA, (2) 0.5 mL Herculase II Fusion Polymerase (Agilent Technologies Cat# 600,677), (3) 0.5 mL 100 mM dNTP Mix, (4) 10 mL Herculase 5X Buffer, (5) 31 mL of PCR-grade water, and (6) 2.5 mL PCR1 F/R 10 mM primer mixture (synthesized by Eurofins Genomics). PCR1 reactions were processed using optimized cycling parameters: 98 °C for 3 min, 22 cycles of 98 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s, and then 72 °C for 3 min. After amplification, PCR1 products for each replicate/condition were pooled purified with the ZYMO DNA Clean & Concentrator-100 kit (Cat# D4030) using the manufacturer's protocol.

L95: Next, 4 parallel PCR2 reactions were performed per replicate (i.e., per pooled PCR1 product) to affix the correct P5/P7 and i5/i7 Illumina adapters while maintaining sufficient library coverage. Individual 50uL reactions were prepared as follows: (1) 2.5 mL PCR1 product, (2) 0.5 mL Herculase II Fusion Polymerase, (3) 0.5 mL 100 mM dNTP Mix, (4) 10 mL Herculase 5X Buffer, (5) 34 mL of PCR-grade water, (6) 2.5 mL PCR2 F/R 10 mM primer mixture (synthesized by Eurofins Genomics). All PCR2 reactions were run using the following thermal cycling program: 95 °C for 3 min, 20 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s, 60 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, and then 72 °C for 3 min. After amplification, parallel PCR2 products were pooled, run on a 2% TAE agarose gel, and extracted with ADB (ZYMO Cat # D4001-1–100) following the manufacturer's protocol. Following purification, PCR2 dsDNA concentrations were measured using PicoGreen (Invitrogen Cat# P11496) adhering to product instructions. After quantification, PCR2 products were pooled at equimolar ratios and sequenced on a HiSeq2000 (Illumina), 2 × 150 bp configuration, with a 30% PhiX DNA spike-in. Following deconvolution, raw FASTQ sequences were processed using the MAGeCK pipeline to generate read counts (Additional File 1: Supplementary Table S7) and identify significantly enriched or depleted genes [ 83 ]. The core essential and non-essential genes utilized for analysis were previously reported from prior CRISPR screens (Additional File 1: Supplementary Table S3) [ 60 ]. Genes were deemed 'essential' if they had Wald-adjusted  p -values < 0.05 and beta scores < −0.3 (indicating significant depletion across biological replicates) from MAGeCK processing.
